Hello, my name is William Gray. This summer, I completed a Penn State Faculty-Assisted Research Project as a short-squeeze research intern. In this video, I would like to show you what I developed over the course of my internship.

My internship focused on short squeezes in the stock market. To understand what a short squeeze is, you first need to understand short selling.

Put simply, there are two main ways to speculate on a stock. If you believe its price will rise, you can purchase shares and profit if their value increases. If you believe its price will fall, you can short the stock.

To short a stock, an investor borrows shares from someone who already owns them and sells those shares at the current market price. The investor must eventually buy the shares back and return them to the lender. If the price falls, the investor can repurchase the shares for less than the original sale price and keep the difference as profit. If the price rises, however, the investor loses money because the shares must be repurchased at a higher price.

A short squeeze can occur when a stock has significant short interest, meaning that a large number of its shares have been sold short, and then experiences a rapid increase in price. As the price rises, short sellers begin losing money and may exit their positions by buying back the shares they borrowed.

This increased buying pressure can push the stock price even higher, forcing additional short sellers to close their positions. The result can be a dramatic and extremely rapid increase in the stock’s price before it eventually declines or levels out.

Several indicators and trends commonly appear in stocks that experience short squeezes:

* A float below 20 million shares
* Relative volume greater than five
* A daily price increase of at least 10 percent
* A positive news catalyst
* A stock price between $2 and $20

Each of these indicators is important for a different reason.

A float below 20 million means that fewer than 20 million shares are readily available for public trading. Stocks with smaller floats are generally more susceptible to volatility because a relatively small increase in buying demand can move the price more quickly and dramatically.

Relative volume greater than five means that the stock’s current trading volume is at least five times its typical volume. This can indicate unusually strong market attention and a large increase in buying or selling activity.

A price increase of at least 10 percent from the previous closing price demonstrates positive momentum. It is generally more useful to identify a stock that is already moving upward than to identify one that meets several other conditions but has not yet shown upward momentum.

A price between $2 and $20 is less of a strict indicator and more of a practical guideline. This range can allow traders to purchase a relatively large number of shares compared with stocks priced at $50 or $100 per share.

Stocks priced below $2 also allow traders to take large positions, but they may be more volatile and more likely to experience trading halts. Because their prices are so low, an increase of only a few cents can represent a dramatic percentage change.

A positive news catalyst is useful because it provides a possible reason for the stock’s price increase. A stock can rise without a clear news catalyst, but that momentum may disappear once sellers catch up or trading resumes after a halt. Positive news may help sustain market attention and provide additional support for the price movement.

My original concept was an application window that displayed a checkmark or an X next to each indicator. At the bottom of the window, the application would display the stock’s short-squeeze potential.

If all five indicators were satisfied, the potential would be classified as high. If four were satisfied, it would be classified as medium. If three or fewer were satisfied, it would be classified as low.

I also created a flowchart showing how I imagined the application would operate. The user would enter a stock ticker, and the application would use that ticker to retrieve market information. It would collect the relevant data, process it, and display the results on the screen.

The application would also use a machine-learning algorithm to analyze news catalysts and determine whether recent news was positive or negative. It would then combine that result with the other indicators and classify the stock’s short-squeeze potential as low, medium, or high.

The greatest difficulty I encountered was analyzing news catalysts. The other indicators are numerical values that can be collected and filtered relatively easily. News is more difficult because the program must determine whether an article is likely to have a positive, neutral, or negative effect on a stock.

To address this problem, I created a machine-learning model using a random forest classifier. I chose a random forest classifier because the task involved classifying news into categories rather than predicting a continuous numerical value.

This is the dataset I used to train the algorithm. Each entry contains a news headline and a corresponding label:

* Positive one indicates positive price movement.
* Zero indicates neutral price movement.
* Negative one indicates negative price movement.

When the news-catalyst analysis script is run, the user can enter a ticker symbol. The program then retrieves the five most recent headlines and displays a prediction, confidence score, timestamp, and source URL for each article.

The URL can be selected to open the original news source.

The program also provides a final prediction summary. This includes a net score that combines the sentiment prediction with the model’s confidence score. It then assigns an overall confidence level of low, medium, high, or very high.

I later adapted this program into a notification-based system. Instead of relying entirely on scraped data, the updated version used an API to retrieve articles.

Each time the program finds an article that satisfies the required conditions, it produces an audible notification. To qualify, the article must receive a confidence score of at least 0.7 and a positive news prediction.

There are several remaining issues and opportunities for improvement.

The first issue is the lack of a news-sentiment column in the primary screener.

I wanted to include sentiment directly alongside each stock, but I encountered several technical problems. Each implementation I attempted required the program to make a separate API request for every stock being filtered.

Because the screener initially evaluates thousands of stocks, this would require thousands of API calls within a very short period. The API would then time out or block the requests because the usage rate was too high.

Another issue involved API-token regeneration.

When the program ran for an extended period, Finviz would sometimes regenerate the API token. I am not certain whether this happened because the service detected unusual API activity or interpreted the requests as unauthorized use.

When the token changed, the application stopped working. I then had to locate the new token, manually replace it in the program, stop the application, and restart it.

A useful improvement would be to create a system that automatically detects when Finviz regenerates the token, retrieves the new token, and updates the application without requiring a manual restart.

Another major objective that I was unable to complete during the summer was developing a live short-interest estimation formula.

Official short-interest data is generally published only twice per month. The data is collected around the 15th and the final day of each month, but it is not released until approximately 10 to 12 days later.

As a result, the available short-interest figures are already outdated by the time they are published.

I wanted to develop a formula that could estimate short interest in real time using more frequently available market data.

When I first began this part of the project, I collected data from only 10 stocks. Those stocks were all relatively large companies and did not meet the typical qualifications for a short squeeze.

This created two problems. First, 10 stocks did not provide enough data. Second, the data was not relevant to the types of low-float, volatile stocks the screener was designed to identify.

I therefore discarded that dataset and started again.

The initial data had also been collected through web scraping, which limited me to approximately 10 tickers. I later switched to an API, which allowed me to collect data from 80 tickers instead.

I could have collected data from additional stocks, but I did not have enough time to observe a sufficient number of official short-interest reporting cycles.

I estimate that I would have needed at least one additional month of data collection to begin developing a reliable short-interest estimation formula.

Beyond resolving these issues, several additional features could improve the application.

The first would be a notification function similar to the news-alert system demonstrated earlier. Instead of producing an audible sound only when a positive headline is detected, the application could send an alert whenever a stock enters the Prime Setup category.

Another possible improvement would be an automated trading feature.

This would be significantly more difficult and much riskier because it would allow the application to directly use a trader’s funds. The system would require extensive testing, validation, and risk controls before it could be trusted to place real trades.

A final feature would be ticker-specific news searching within the Breaking News section.

For example, a search bar could allow the user to enter a ticker symbol and determine whether a positively classified article had been published about that company during the current trading day.
