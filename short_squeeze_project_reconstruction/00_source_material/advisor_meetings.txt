Here is a simple example.

Suppose Tesla is trading at $400 per share, and I believe its price will decline. I borrow 50 shares and immediately sell them.

Fifty shares multiplied by $400 equals $20,000, so I receive $20,000 from the sale.

I do this because I expect Tesla’s price to fall to $300.

If the price falls to $300, I can repurchase the 50 shares for $15,000 and return them to the lender. The difference between the $20,000 sale and the $15,000 repurchase is my profit.

Short selling is therefore not simply about borrowing money. It involves borrowing shares, selling them immediately, hoping their price declines, buying them back at a lower price, and returning them to the lender.

However, suppose I am wrong and positive news causes Tesla’s price to rise to $500.

Because I borrowed the shares at a time when they were worth $400, I may rush to repurchase them before the price rises further. If I have to buy them back at $500, I will lose money.

When many investors have bet against the same company and unexpectedly positive news causes its price to rise, they may all rush to buy shares so they can close their short positions.

Because they are competing with one another to purchase the stock, the additional buying pressure can cause a short squeeze.

Some short squeezes can produce price increases of several hundred percent. They can be extremely dramatic.

Ross Cameron, for example, is a trader who frequently focuses on short-squeeze opportunities.
